Summary

This project asked whether information known when a hotel booking is made can predict whether that booking will be cancelled. Using 119,390 bookings from two hotel types, the analysis found that logistic regression ranked cancellations substantially better than a majority-class baseline and could identify more cancellations than the baseline at the final held-out evaluation. The hotel could therefore use the model to prioritize bookings for cancellation-risk follow-up, while accepting a trade-off between catching cancellations and contacting bookings that remain active. The main limitations are that the study covers two hotels and a limited period, includes potentially duplicate records, uses a random rather than time-based test split, and chooses a threshold for equal precision and recall rather than hotel-specific costs.

Introduction

Hotel cancellations create uncertainty for room inventory, staffing, and revenue management. When a booking is cancelled after an inventory decision or guest-arrival plan has been made, the hotel may lose a room opportunity or spend resources on guests who are unlikely to cancel. A useful cancellation-prediction model could flag high-risk reservations for targeted communication, revised deposit or payment policies, or a carefully managed overbooking strategy. The value of such a model depends on both its ability to rank bookings by risk and the consequences of any intervention, because not every hotel action is appropriate for every false alarm.

This report answers the following question: Can booking information available at reservation time predict whether a hotel booking will be cancelled, and how does changing the decision threshold affect the usefulness of the prediction? The question is actionable if the response variable is available for past bookings and the predictors are available before cancellation occurs. It is measurable with the available data because the response is a binary cancellation indicator and the predictors describe individual reservations.

The Hotel Booking Demand dataset was originally published by Antonio, Almeida, and Nunes and is publicly distributed through Kaggle [1,2]. Each row represents one booking made for a city hotel or resort hotel. The original source contains 119,390 rows and variables describing booking timing, length of stay, guest composition, deposit and market information, previous cancellations, and customer characteristics [1,2]. The variables used in this analysis describe information available at or near the booking stage, while the following variables were excluded: reservation status and reservation status date reveal the final outcome; assigned room type, booking changes, and days in waiting list may reflect events after the initial booking. The target variable is whether the booking was cancelled, coded as 1 for cancellation and 0 for no cancellation. The target supports binary classification and is not evenly balanced: 44,224 bookings, or 37.0%, were cancelled. These features and the target are sufficient to evaluate whether past patterns can predict cancellation in a held-out booking sample.

Methods

Overview of the analysis

The goal of this analysis is to predict whether a hotel booking will be cancelled (is_canceled) using information available about the booking. We used the Hotel Booking Demand dataset from Kaggle, which contains 119,390 bookings from a city hotel and a resort hotel. Of these, 44,224 (37.0%) were cancelled and 75,166 (63.0%) were not.

We first removed five columns. reservation_status and reservation_status_date record the outcome of the booking itself, so keeping them would let the model “see” the answer (target leakage). assigned_room_type, booking_changes and days_in_waiting_list are recorded after the booking is made, so they would not be available when predicting whether a new booking will cancel. The remaining 26 predictors were prepared as follows: missing values in numeric columns were replaced with the median and the columns were standardized, missing values in categorical columns were labelled “Unknown” and the columns were one-hot encoded, and the agent and company ID columns were treated as categories, with missing IDs coded as -1. After encoding there were 929 features.

We then split the data into 80% training (95,512 bookings) and 20% test (23,878 bookings), stratified by the target so both sets keep the 37% cancellation rate. All preprocessing was placed inside a scikit-learn pipeline together with the classifier, so imputation, scaling and encoding are learned from training data only and the test set cannot influence them. The test set was kept untouched until the very end.

The remaining steps were:

  1. Fit a baseline model and a logistic regression model and compare them using 5-fold cross-validation on the training set.
  2. Select the final model using the cross-validation results only.
  3. Choose a decision threshold using out-of-fold predictions on the training set.
  4. Evaluate the final model once on the held-out test set.

Evaluation metrics

We report accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC and average precision (AP), but different metrics play different roles:

  • Average precision (primary metric for model selection). The practical use of this model is to flag bookings that are likely to cancel so the hotel can act on them. AP summarizes the trade-off between precision (how many flagged bookings really cancel) and recall (how many cancellations we catch) across all possible thresholds, and it focuses on the cancellation class. It does not depend on a particular threshold, so it measures how well the model ranks bookings by risk. Because the baseline’s AP equals the positive rate (0.370), it also has an easy-to-read reference point.

  • F1 (metric for choosing the threshold). Once the model ranks bookings, we must decide how high the predicted probability has to be before a booking is flagged. We chose the threshold that maximizes F1, which weights precision and recall equally. This is a neutral assumption, because we do not have the hotel’s actual costs of a missed cancellation versus a false alarm.

  • Precision, recall, ROC-AUC and accuracy (supporting metrics). Precision and recall show the two kinds of error directly. ROC-AUC is reported as a second threshold-free measure of ranking quality. With a 37% positive rate it is not badly inflated, so it agrees with AP. Accuracy is reported for completeness, but it is a weak headline metric here because the do-nothing baseline already reaches 63.0%.

Models

We compared two models:

  • Baseline (majority-class dummy classifier). This predicts “not cancelled” for every booking. It does not learn anything, but it gives a floor that any useful model must beat, and it shows why accuracy alone is misleading here (it already scores 63.0%).

  • Logistic regression. This is a standard, well-understood classifier for a binary outcome. It outputs a probability of cancellation for each booking, which we need in order to rank bookings by risk and to adjust the decision threshold. It trains quickly even with about 930 features, and its coefficients show which booking characteristics raise or lower cancellation risk, which makes the results easier to explain to a hotel manager. We used it as our main model because it is a strong, interpretable starting point for a tabular problem with many categorical variables.

models = {
    "Dummy (majority class)": make_pipeline(
        clone(preprocessor),
        DummyClassifier(strategy="most_frequent"),
    ),
    "Logistic Regression": make_pipeline(
        clone(preprocessor),
        LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000, random_state=RANDOM_STATE),
    ),
}

Final evaluation (held-out test set, used once)

def evaluate(model, X, y, threshold=0.5):
    proba = model.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]
    pred = (proba >= threshold).astype(int)
    return {
        "accuracy": accuracy_score(y, pred),
        "precision": precision_score(y, pred, zero_division=0),
        "recall": recall_score(y, pred),
        "f1": f1_score(y, pred),
        "roc_auc": roc_auc_score(y, proba),
        "pr_auc": average_precision_score(y, proba),
    }

final_model.fit(X_train, y_train)
dummy = clone(models["Dummy (majority class)"]).fit(X_train, y_train)

test_results = pd.DataFrame({
    "Dummy (0.5)": evaluate(dummy, X_test, y_test),
    f"{cv_best} (0.5)": evaluate(final_model, X_test, y_test),
    f"{cv_best} ({threshold})": evaluate(final_model, X_test, y_test, threshold),
}).T.round(3)

Results

Figures 1 through 4 summarize the training-set cancellation patterns that motivated the modelling analysis. Figure 1 establishes the class imbalance, while Figures 2 through 4 show how cancellation rates vary by lead time, deposit type, arrival month, and hotel type. Table 1 and Figure 5 compare the model candidates, and Table 2 and Figure 6 describe the threshold trade-off before the final held-out evaluation in Table 3.

The main finding is that booking attributes can support useful cancellation-risk ranking even though the majority-class baseline has misleadingly high accuracy. The held-out results show that the selected logistic regression model outperforms the baseline across the threshold-dependent and threshold-independent metrics, and that lowering the decision threshold changes the balance between missed cancellations and false alarms.

Model comparison

Table 1 shows the 5-fold cross-validation results on the training set. The baseline reaches 63.0% accuracy only by predicting that no booking cancels, so its precision, recall and F1 are all 0, its ROC-AUC is 0.500 (no discrimination) and its AP of 0.370 equals the cancellation rate. Logistic regression is far better on every measure, with an AP of 0.868, a ROC-AUC of 0.908 and an F1 of 0.749. Because AP was our selection metric, logistic regression was selected as the final model.

Threshold selection

At the default threshold of 0.5, logistic regression has a precision of 0.806 but a recall of only 0.700, so it misses about 3 in 10 cancellations. Using out-of-fold predictions on the training set, we examined thresholds from 0.20 to 0.80. Lowering the threshold increases recall and lowers precision, and F1 was highest at 0.35 (precision 0.725, recall 0.825, F1 0.771 in the training data).

Test set performance

Table 3 shows the final results on the held-out test set. At the default threshold, the test results closely match the cross-validation estimates (AP 0.865 vs. 0.868, ROC-AUC 0.907 vs. 0.908, F1 0.748 vs. 0.749), so the model does not appear to be overfitted. With the threshold of 0.35 chosen on training data, the test set shows the same pattern: recall rises from 0.697 to 0.817 and F1 from 0.748 to 0.769, while precision falls from 0.808 to 0.726 and accuracy falls slightly from 0.826 to 0.818. AP and ROC-AUC are unchanged because they do not depend on the threshold.

Best model

The best model is logistic regression with a decision threshold of 0.35. It clearly outperforms the baseline, generalizes well to unseen bookings, and catches about 82% of cancellations, compared with about 70% at the default threshold. The cost is more false alarms: about 27% of flagged bookings do not cancel, compared with about 19% at 0.5. We consider this a reasonable trade if an unsold room caused by a missed cancellation is more costly to the hotel than a flagged booking that turns out to show up. If the hotel instead wants to be more certain that flagged bookings will cancel, a higher threshold, such as 0.5, is more appropriate.

Discussion

These results answer the stated question by showing that information available at booking time can identify likely cancellations, and that the useful threshold depends on the hotel’s operational priorities. The findings are consistent with the training patterns in Figures 1 through 4, where cancellation frequency varies across booking lead time, deposit type, and temporal or hotel characteristics. It was reasonable to expect some ranking advantage over the baseline because cancellation behavior differs across these booking attributes, although the size of the difference at held-out evaluation was favorable.

The real-world meaning is that a hotel could use the model to rank reservations by cancellation risk rather than making a binary decision about every booking. At the 0.5 threshold, the model catches fewer cancellations but is more certain that a flagged booking is likely to cancel. At 0.35, it catches more cancellations but also flags more bookings that remain active. This makes the threshold decision a business question: the hotel should prefer the lower threshold if a missed cancellation is more costly, and the higher threshold if unnecessary intervention or a false alarm is more costly.

Limitations

  • Only one model besides the baseline was compared, so we cannot say logistic regression is the best possible model. Logistic regression assumes linear effects on the log-odds and may miss interactions between features (for example, lead time and deposit type). A tree-based model such as gradient boosting is the natural next step.
  • The threshold was chosen to maximize F1, which is an assumption of equal importance for precision and recall, not a cost derived from the hotel’s business.
  • The dataset contains duplicate rows, and copies may appear in both the training and test sets, so the scores may be slightly optimistic.
  • The data come from two hotels over a limited time period, and we used a random split rather than a time-based split. Performance on future bookings, or at other hotels, may be lower.

If deployed, the model could affect guest communication, staffing, inventory control, and revenue-management decisions. At a lower threshold, a hotel may contact more at-risk guests or hold inventory more conservatively, but it will also spend resources on bookings that would have remained active. A higher threshold reduces this false-alert burden but misses more cancellations. The appropriate operating point should therefore be based on documented cancellation costs and the cost of intervention, rather than F1 alone [4,5].

Future work could compare time-based validation with the random split, repeat the analysis after carefully removing duplicate records, and test nonlinear or cost-sensitive models. It could also estimate expected profit or room opportunity cost for each threshold, add pricing and inventory information, and evaluate whether the model remains accurate for a later period or a hotel outside this dataset [1,3,5].
